\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Detection, per attack class}
\label{sec:res-detect}

Table~\ref{tab:per-attack} and Fig.~\ref{fig:per-attack} report detection
per attack class. Both the deployed model and its leakage-free retraining catch
eight of the ten classes almost perfectly---every Mirai attack, and BASHLITE's
scan, junk and combo---yet miss nearly all of BASHLITE's TCP and UDP floods:
the deployed model detects \NdeplTcp{} and \NdeplUdp{} of them, and the
retrained model \NlstmTcp{} and \NlstmUdp{} over five seeds. Because these two
classes make up \NblindShare{} of all \NattackWindows{} attack windows, the
overall detection rate is only \NdeplTPR{} for the deployed model and
\NlstmTPR{} for the retrained one, a figure that no aggregate accuracy on the
remaining classes would hint at.

The miss is not a matter of a badly placed threshold. The retrained model
separates the other eight classes from benign traffic with an AUC of
\NotherAUC{}, but the two BASHLITE floods with an AUC of only \NblindAUC{}.
Lowering the threshold far enough to catch half of their windows would raise
the false-positive rate to \NblindCostFPRin{} on the training devices and
\NblindCostFPRall{} across all devices. For the deployed model the diagnosis
is especially stark: every window of these floods receives almost the same
reconstruction error, just below its threshold and inside the range of ordinary
benign variation. Setting a separate threshold per device, which we also tried,
recovered them on one device only.

The two baselines locate the cause. The dense autoencoder of N-BaIoT's
authors, which scores rows independently and models no order, misses the two
floods entirely (\NdenseTcp{} and \NdenseUdp{}), and so does the Isolation
Forest; the blind spot belongs to reconstruction- and isolation-based detection
on these features, not to the LSTM. Elsewhere the dense model matches the
sequence model: the same overall detection (\NdenseTPR{} against \NlstmTPR{})
and the same false-positive rate on unseen devices (\NdenseFPRout{} against
\NlstmFPRout{}), with half the false positives on the training devices
(\NdenseFPRin{} against \NlstmFPRin{}) and a higher AUC on unseen devices
(\NdenseAUCout{} against \NlstmAUCout{}). On this data, modelling packet order
adds cost without a measurable benefit. The Isolation Forest raises the fewest
false alarms on unseen devices (\NisoFPRout{}) only because it detects so
little: \NisoTPR{} of attack windows at the same nominal budget, although its
AUC of \NisoAUCout{} shows that its ranking is better than its operating point.

\subsection{Unseen devices}
\label{sec:res-heldout}

Evaluating on devices absent from training raises the pooled false-positive
rate of the retrained model from \NlstmFPRin{} on the training devices to
\NlstmFPRout{} on the held-out ones (the deployed model reaches \NdeplFPRout{}
on them). The increase is not uniform. The held-out \emph{Ennio} doorbell is
flagged as rarely as the training devices, while the \emph{Philips} baby
monitor, whose benign traffic is the largest of any device in the dataset,
produces most of the false alarms. A single threshold calibrated
on a household's existing devices thus says little about the next device
added to it, and a single device with unusual traffic can dominate the false-alarm budget of the whole network.

\subsection{Live feature parity}
\label{sec:res-parity}

Offline results describe the model on N-BaIoT's features; they describe the
running system only if the live extractor computes the same features. We
checked this with invariants measured on N-BaIoT's own rows, which any faithful
extractor must satisfy, on \NparPackets{} real packets from the Mirai capture
distributed with Kitsune~\cite{kitsunepy} (Table~\ref{tab:parity}). The
extractor originally shipped with \textsc{Sentinel} fed a single stream to both
sides of every two-dimensional statistic, so its correlation features took only
\NparOldPccN{} distinct values and its radius violated the bound on most
packets. After replacing it with the reference statistics engine and pinning
the stream layout to what the data shows, every invariant holds and the
correlation features take \NparNewPccN{} distinct values within $[-1,1]$.

\subsection{LLM classification}
\label{sec:res-llm}

Table~\ref{tab:llm} summarises the pre-registered experiment. With the prompt
\textsc{Sentinel} actually sends, the model named an acceptable ATT\&CK
technique for \NllmAsbTech{} of the 200 attack windows and an acceptable
tactic for \NllmAsbTac{}, although every response was valid JSON. Adding the
traffic summary did not rescue it: \NllmEviTech{} for the technique and
\NllmEviTac{} for the tactic. The pre-registered expectation that evidence
would help is therefore not supported at this model size. Restricting to the
windows the detector would actually forward changes nothing
(\NllmAsbTechFlag{} as built).

The failure has a consistent shape. Across all \NllmN{} responses,
\NllmCtwoShare{} name Command and Control as the tactic, typically technique
T1071 (Application Layer Protocol), and not one names Impact, the tactic for
denial of service, although 320 of them describe floods. Given a device, a remote address and a high anomaly score, the model
describes a compromised device talking to a controller---a plausible prior,
and wrong for both scans and floods. Its reading of the evidence follows the
same prior: a scan's many short connections become ``a high volume of
connections \ldots\ potentially indicative of C2 communication.'' Accuracy did
not differ between the calls run locally (\NllmLocTech{}, $n=\NllmLocN$) and on
the cloud GPU (\NllmCloudTech{}, $n=\NllmCloudN$).

Enforcement nevertheless happens. \NllmSevNineShare{} of responses assign
severity 9 or more, and under \textsc{Sentinel}'s policy the pipeline would
have blocked \NllmAsbBlock{} of the attack windows as built (exploratory
analysis). The block is thus decided by a severity the model assigns almost
regardless of what the traffic is, and the ATT\&CK label attached to it is
nearly always wrong. We did not query the model on benign windows, but nothing
in its behaviour suggests it would treat a detector false positive differently.

\subsection{Latency}
\label{sec:res-latency}

Table~\ref{tab:latency} gives the median time of each stage. Everything the
appliance computes itself is fast: extracting features and scoring a window
together take \NlatDetectPkt{}\,ms per packet, enough for roughly
\NdetectPPS{} packets per second on one core, and the bus hop and response
policy each cost under a millisecond. Enforcing a decision is also quick: an
nftables block takes \NenfBlock{}\,ms at the median (95th percentile
\NenfBlockP{}\,ms). The path is dominated by two calls. The
LLM needs \NllmLatMed{}\,s per event (95th percentile \NllmLatP{}\,s), and
\NllmCold{}\,s for the first event after the model service starts. The
AlienVault OTX lookup is slower and far less predictable: a median of
\NotxFullMed{}\,s over \NotxFullN{} lookups, ranging from \NotxFullMin{}\,s to
\NotxFullMax{}\,s. It is made with a synchronous client inside the
classifier's event loop and without a timeout, so while it waits no other
event is classified. The classifier reads only the \emph{general} section of
the OTX response, yet requests every section; requesting that section alone
takes \NotxGenMed{}\,s (median of \NotxGenN{}), a \NotxSpeedup$\times$
reduction.
